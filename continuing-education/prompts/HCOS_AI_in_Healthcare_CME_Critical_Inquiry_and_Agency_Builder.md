# HCOS™ AI in Healthcare CME Critical Inquiry and Agency Builder

## Purpose

Use this prompt to transform an article, research paper, case study, policy development, technology announcement, healthcare incident, or emerging AI concern into clear, balanced, and practical continuing medical education content.

The educational purpose is not merely to describe artificial intelligence.

The purpose is to help healthcare professionals:

- understand what is changing;
- recognize how AI-related problems may develop;
- identify the system conditions contributing to failure;
- ask better questions before accepting AI-generated information or recommendations;
- protect patients, healthcare workers, and organizations;
- distinguish appropriate reliance from inappropriate delegation;
- respond constructively when an AI-enabled system behaves unexpectedly;
- preserve meaningful human judgment, responsibility, and agency during the transition to AI-enabled healthcare.

The education should help reduce preventable suffering by making AI-related risks understandable, visible, and actionable.

- human factors;

- ethics and governance;

- human-centered system design.

Approach the topic through the HCOS™ philosophy:

> Technology exists to serve people.  
> Healthy AI systems are ultimately healthy human systems.

Do not present AI as inherently good, inherently harmful, or inevitable.

Do not treat every AI failure as a technical failure.

Examine how technology, data, people, workflows, policies, incentives, resources, authority, and organizational conditions interact.

# Core Educational Intent

Build the education around this central question:

> What do healthcare professionals need to understand, notice, question, and do so that AI-related change does not unnecessarily reduce human agency, patient safety, professional judgment, or the ability of people to receive compassionate care?

The final material must move learners through five stages:

1. **Understand what is happening.**
2. **Recognize why it matters in healthcare.**
3. **Identify what could cause the system to go wrong.**
4. **Ask the questions that reveal hidden risks or assumptions.**
5. **Know what actions remain available to them.**

# Step 1 — Establish the Professional Practice Gap

Identify the difference between:

- what healthcare professionals currently understand or do; and
- what they need to understand or do in an AI-enabled environment.

State the gap without blaming individual clinicians or leaders.

Use this format:

**Current state:**  
What professionals may currently believe, overlook, misunderstand, or lack the ability to evaluate.

**Desired state:**  
What professionals should be able to recognize, question, decide, communicate, or escalate after completing the education.

**Underlying educational need:**  
Classify the need as one or more of the following:

- knowledge;
- competence;
- performance;
- system or organizational capability.

Explain why this gap matters to patient care, workforce well-being, organizational reliability, or public trust.

# Step 2 — Explain What Happened

Provide a concise, neutral explanation of the source material.

Answer:

- What happened?
- What technology or AI capability is involved?
- What problem was the technology intended to solve?
- Who may benefit?
- Who may carry the risks or burdens?
- What assumptions are being challenged?
- What remains uncertain?
- What claims are supported by evidence?
- What claims are interpretations, predictions, or opinions?

Do not repeat sensational headlines without examining them.

When the source is a social media post, commentary, press release, or news article, treat it as a signal to investigate rather than as conclusive evidence.

Whenever research capability is available:

- verify major claims using primary or authoritative sources;
- prioritize peer-reviewed research, government information, professional guidance, technical documentation, and original reports;
- identify conflicting evidence;
- include publication dates;
- indicate when evidence is preliminary, indirect, or rapidly evolving.

# Step 3 — Separate Evidence, Interpretation, and Forecast

Organize important claims into three categories.

## Established or Reasonably Supported

What is supported by reliable evidence or directly documented facts?

## Interpretation

What conclusions are being drawn from those facts?

## Architectural Forecast

What future development appears plausible but has not yet been established?

Use language that reflects the strength of the evidence.

Avoid presenting forecasts as certainties.

Explicitly identify:

- known facts;
- reasonable inferences;
- contested interpretations;
- unanswered questions;
- speculative scenarios.

# Step 5 — Analyze How the AI System Could Go Haywire

Do not stop at statements such as “the AI made a mistake.”

Identify the interacting conditions that could create or amplify the problem.

Analyze the following possible contributing domains.

## 1. Data

- incomplete data;
- inaccurate data;
- outdated information;
- unrepresentative training data;
- missing clinical context;
- documentation errors;
- mislabeled information;
- inaccessible information;
- data that changes over time;
- inappropriate reuse of data.

## 2. Model or Technical Design

- hallucination or unsupported output;
- poor calibration;
- brittleness;
- lack of explainability;
- model drift;
- inappropriate optimization;
- weak testing;
- integration failure;
- cybersecurity vulnerability;
- inability to recognize unfamiliar situations.

## 3. Identity and Context

- wrong patient;
- wrong clinician;
- wrong role;
- wrong organization;
- merged identities;
- failure to distinguish current from historical information;
- failure to recognize who has authority to act.

## 4. Workflow

- AI inserted at the wrong point;
- duplicate work;
- poorly designed handoffs;
- alerts without action pathways;
- recommendations separated from relevant context;
- no process for exceptions;
- time pressure that encourages automatic acceptance.

## 5. Human Factors

- automation bias;
- overconfidence;
- misunderstanding system limitations;
- alert fatigue;
- cognitive overload;
- inadequate training;
- fear of challenging the technology;
- assumption that another person verified the output.

## 6. Authority and Delegation

- unclear decision rights;
- AI acting beyond its approved role;
- humans retaining responsibility without meaningful control;
- inability to override the system;
- recommendations being mistaken for orders;
- tasks delegated without appropriate supervision.

## 7. Governance

- unclear accountability;
- absent policies;
- inadequate validation;
- weak monitoring;
- no incident reporting pathway;
- insufficient patient representation;
- lack of independent oversight;
- failure to reassess the technology after implementation.

## 8. Economics and Incentives

- pressure to reduce staffing;
- productivity targets;
- cost reduction prioritized over safety;
- hidden infrastructure or maintenance costs;
- vendor lock-in;
- reimbursement incentives;
- pressure to deploy before readiness.

## 9. Organizational Conditions

- inadequate resources;
- fragmented leadership;
- poor psychological safety;
- insufficient recovery time;
- lack of technical support;
- communication failures;
- implementation during periods of excessive system strain.

## 10. External Environment

- regulatory change;
- cyberattack;
- technology outages;
- supply constraints;
- rapidly changing evidence;
- public misinformation;
- vendor or infrastructure failure.

Explain which factors appear most relevant to the topic.

Show how several small weaknesses may interact to produce a major failure.

# Step 6 — Construct a Causal Pathway

Create a simple sequence showing how harm could emerge.

Use this structure:

**System condition**  
↓  
**AI behavior or limitation**  
↓  
**Human interpretation or workflow response**  
↓  
**Decision or action**  
↓  
**Potential patient, workforce, or organizational consequence**

Then identify where the pathway could be interrupted.

For each interruption point, specify:

- who could act;
- what they could notice;
- what question they could ask;
- what authority they would need;
- what system support should be available.

# Step 7 — Develop Critical Thinking Questions

Create questions throughout the activity, not only at the end.

Questions must encourage interpretation, judgment, and application rather than simple recall.

For each major section, provide one or more of the following question types.

## Recognition Questions

Help learners notice that a potential problem exists.

Examples:

- What is the first indication that this system may not be functioning as intended?
- Which information would you verify before relying on this output?
- What important context may be missing?
- Who might experience a risk that is not immediately visible?

## Causal Questions

Help learners examine why the problem may be occurring.

Examples:

- Is this primarily a model failure, a data failure, a workflow failure, or an interaction among several conditions?
- What organizational pressure may be shaping how the AI is being used?
- What happened before the AI produced the concerning output?
- Could the system be functioning as designed while still creating harm?

## Authority Questions

Help learners determine who may decide, override, or intervene.

Examples:

- Who has authority to accept, reject, or modify this recommendation?
- Does the person carrying responsibility have meaningful control?
- Has decision-making authority been delegated explicitly or merely assumed?
- What should never be delegated without human review?

## Evidence Questions

Help learners assess the quality and limits of information.

Examples:

- What evidence supports this claim?
- Does the evidence apply to this patient, population, or setting?
- What remains unknown?
- Is a prediction being presented as an established fact?
- How recent is the information being used?

## Human Factors Questions

Help learners examine how people interact with the technology.

Examples:

- What makes a clinician more likely to accept this output automatically?
- Does the workflow create enough time and information for meaningful review?
- Could alert fatigue or cognitive overload affect the decision?
- Are workers psychologically safe enough to question the system?

## Patient-Centered Questions

Help learners preserve dignity, autonomy, and compassionate care.

Examples:

- Does the patient know that AI influenced this decision?
- What opportunity does the patient have to ask questions or object?
- Whose needs are centered in the current design?
- Could this process create unequal burdens for certain patients?
- What human conversation remains necessary?

## Leadership Questions

Help leaders examine system responsibility.

Examples:

- What conditions did leadership create around this technology?
- What outcome is the system being optimized to achieve?
- What is being measured, and what is not being measured?
- How will leaders know whether the system is creating hidden work or harm?
- What happens when frontline professionals disagree with the AI?
- Who reviews patterns across multiple incidents?

## Forward-Looking Questions

Help professionals prepare for future developments.

Examples:

- What capability could this system be given next?
- What new authority might be delegated over time?
- Which safeguards would need to exist before expanding its role?
- What human capability should be preserved even if the AI becomes more accurate?
- What form of dependence could be difficult to reverse?

---

# Step 8 — Provide the Answer and Rationale

After each critical thinking question, provide:

1. **Best response**
2. **Why it is the best response**
3. **Why other plausible responses may be incomplete**
4. **What information remains uncertain**
5. **What the healthcare professional can do next**

Do not create trick questions.

Avoid answer choices where one option is obviously more detailed than the others.

For multiple-choice questions:

- include one best answer;
- include three plausible distractors;
- provide a rationale for every option;
- test application or judgment;
- avoid “all of the above” and “none of the above” unless educationally necessary.

# Step 11 — Create the CME Learning Objectives

Write two to four measurable objectives.

Use action verbs such as:

- identify;
- distinguish;
- evaluate;
- analyze;
- apply;
- recognize;
- formulate;
- select;
- demonstrate;
- develop.

Avoid vague verbs such as:

- know;
- learn;
- appreciate;
- become familiar with;
- understand, unless paired with an observable action.

At least one objective should address knowledge.

At least one objective should address competence or decision-making.

At least one objective should address practical application, leadership, or system improvement when appropriate.

Example structure:

> After completing this activity, participants should be able to:

1. Identify the technical, human, and organizational conditions that may contribute to AI-related failure in healthcare.
2. Distinguish between an AI recommendation, a clinical decision, and an authorized action.
3. Apply a structured set of questions when evaluating an unexpected or potentially unsafe AI-generated output.
4. Recommend one organizational safeguard that supports responsible AI use and meaningful human oversight.

# Step 13 — Create a Case-Based Scenario

Develop one realistic healthcare scenario related to the topic.

Include:

- the care setting;
- the patient or operational situation;
- the AI system’s role;
- the information available to the professional;
- the information that is missing;
- the organizational pressure affecting the decision;
- the moment at which professional judgment is required.

Do not make the error obvious at the beginning.

Ask learners what they would do next.

Then walk through:

1. what should be verified;
2. who should be involved;
3. what should be documented;
4. whether the output should be accepted, modified, rejected, or escalated;
5. what system issue should be reported;
6. what leadership should learn from the event.

Protect patient privacy and avoid unnecessary identifying details.

# Step 14 — Create the Assessment

## Embedded Questions

## Post-Activity Questions

Create three to five questions aligned directly with the learning objectives.

At least one question should involve:

- recognition of an AI-related risk;
- evaluation of evidence or missing context;
- authority or delegation;
- selection of an appropriate professional or leadership response.

## Commitment-to-Change Question

Ask:

> Based on this activity, what is one question, safeguard, or practice you intend to introduce when evaluating AI in your professional setting?

## Confidence Question

Ask learners to rate their confidence before and after the activity in their ability to:

- evaluate an AI-generated output;
- identify contributing system conditions;
- recognize when human escalation is necessary;
- discuss an AI risk with leadership.

# Step 15 — Preserve Scientific and Educational Integrity

The final activity must:

- be accurate;
- be balanced;
- be based on the best reasonably available evidence;
- distinguish evidence from opinion;
- acknowledge meaningful uncertainty;
- avoid unsupported clinical recommendations;
- avoid presenting a product, vendor, or commercial entity as the preferred solution;
- use generic names when possible;
- disclose relevant product or company names only when necessary to understand the case;
- avoid promotional language;
- identify financial or professional conflicts that require review;
- present reasonable alternative interpretations when evidence is disputed.

Do not state or imply that the activity is accredited.

Use the term **CME-ready educational draft** until it has been reviewed and approved by the appropriate accredited provider.

# Step 16 — Conduct a Human-Centered Quality Review

Before finalizing the activity, evaluate it using the following questions.

## Clarity

- Can a healthcare professional without an AI background understand it?
- Are technical terms defined?
- Is the central systems lesson clear?

## Balance

- Are both potential benefits and risks discussed?
- Is uncertainty visible?
- Are alternative interpretations represented fairly?

## Practicality

- Does the learner know what to do differently?
- Are the recommended actions realistic?
- Does the activity provide more than generic advice?

## Human Agency

- Does the activity clarify what humans remain responsible for?
- Does it show how professionals can question, override, report, or escalate?
- Does it avoid telling professionals simply to trust the technology?

## System Responsibility

- Does the activity examine organizational conditions rather than blaming individuals?
- Does it identify the responsibilities of leadership?
- Does it recognize hidden workload or implementation burden?

## Patient Protection

- Are dignity, safety, autonomy, equity, and compassionate care addressed?
- Are patients treated as people rather than data inputs?
- Does the activity consider who may be excluded or disproportionately harmed?

## Educational Alignment

- Does every assessment question align with a learning objective?
- Does the narration support the slide rather than repeat it?
- Are critical thinking questions placed at meaningful moments?
- Is the post-activity evaluation capable of identifying a change in knowledge, competence, confidence, or intended practice?

Revise any section that does not meet these standards.

# Tone and Language

Use a tone that is:

- calm;
- credible;
- conversational;
- nonjudgmental;
- curious;
- evidence-aware;
- practical;
- respectful of professional expertise;
- protective of patients and healthcare workers.

Do not use fear to create engagement.

Do not imply that healthcare professionals are deficient because they do not understand the technical details of AI.

The goal is to build discernment, preparedness, and agency.

End with this central message:

> Healthcare professionals do not need to become AI engineers. They do need the ability to recognize when an AI-enabled system may be operating without sufficient context, evidence, oversight, or human support—and to know what questions and actions can help protect the people depending on that system.
