# Evidence Search and Verification

## Purpose

This document defines a structured process for identifying, evaluating, and verifying evidence used in CME educational content.

The objective is not to attach a citation to every sentence as quickly as possible.

The objective is to determine whether the proposed source actually supports the **exact educational claim** being made.

---

## 1. Begin With the Claim

Before searching, state the claim as precisely as possible.

Ask:

1. What exactly is being asserted?
2. Is the statement factual, interpretive, inferential, or recommendation-based?
3. Does the statement contain more than one independently verifiable proposition?
4. What population, intervention, comparator, outcome, setting, and timeframe are implied?
5. What type of evidence would appropriately support the claim?

When necessary, split compound statements into atomic claims before searching.

---

## 2. Classify the Evidence Need

Determine whether the claim requires evidence involving:

- epidemiology;
- diagnosis;
- prognosis;
- treatment efficacy;
- treatment safety;
- comparative effectiveness;
- guideline recommendations;
- systematic review or meta-analysis;
- implementation;
- healthcare disparities;
- access;
- patient-reported outcomes;
- healthcare utilization;
- clinical workflow;
- expert consensus;
- emerging evidence.

Do not assume the same source type is appropriate for every claim.

---

## 3. Prioritize Appropriate Source Types

Depending on the claim, relevant sources may include:

1. current professional guidelines;
2. systematic reviews and meta-analyses;
3. randomized controlled trials;
4. prospective or retrospective observational studies;
5. diagnostic accuracy studies;
6. safety surveillance or pharmacovigilance evidence;
7. high-quality consensus statements;
8. authoritative regulatory or public-health sources;
9. implementation studies;
10. other primary research directly relevant to the proposition.

Source hierarchy should not be applied mechanically.

A guideline may be the most appropriate source for a recommendation, while a randomized trial may be more appropriate for a specific efficacy estimate.

---

## 4. Verify the Source

Before using a source, confirm:

- the source exists;
- authorship and publication information are accurate;
- the publication is the intended source;
- the population is relevant;
- the intervention and comparator are relevant;
- the outcome matches the claim;
- the timeframe is appropriate;
- the study design is understood;
- important limitations are known;
- the source has not been superseded where recency materially matters.

Do not rely only on abstracts, search snippets, secondary summaries, or AI-generated descriptions when the underlying source can reasonably be reviewed.

---

## 5. Test Claim–Source Entailment

Ask:

> If a reviewer opened this source, would the source reasonably justify the exact proposition written in the educational material?

A source is inadequate when it merely discusses the same disease, drug, intervention, or concept without supporting the specific claim.

### Adequate Support

The source directly supports the proposition within a relevant context.

### Partial Support

The source supports only part of the sentence.

Action:

- split the statement;
- narrow the wording;
- qualify the conclusion;
- or identify additional evidence.

### Indirect Support

The source supports a related concept but requires an inferential step.

Action:

- identify the inference;
- determine whether the inference is educationally appropriate;
- and label or qualify it when material.

### No Adequate Support

Use:

`[SOURCE VERIFICATION REQUIRED]`

Do not solve the problem by substituting a plausible-looking citation.

---

## 6. Preserve Evidence Type

When integrating evidence, preserve whether the source represents:

- established evidence;
- guideline recommendation;
- primary research finding;
- systematic review or meta-analysis;
- consensus or expert opinion;
- emerging evidence;
- clinical interpretation;
- educational synthesis;
- inference;
- uncertainty or evidence gap.

Do not flatten these categories into a generic statement of fact.

---

## 7. Preserve Evidence Boundaries

Do not automatically convert:

- association into causation;
- correlation into mechanism;
- a single study into consensus;
- surrogate improvement into demonstrated patient benefit;
- statistical significance into clinical significance;
- biological plausibility into clinical effectiveness;
- absence of evidence into evidence of absence;
- guideline recommendation into universal requirement;
- expert opinion into established evidence;
- common practice into evidence-based best practice;
- educational synthesis into a source-established conclusion;
- several compatible sources into proof of the entire narrative.

---

## 8. Check Recency and Temporal Provenance

For clinically evolving topics, ask:

- Is the guideline still current?
- Has a newer guideline replaced or materially altered the recommendation?
- Has subsequent evidence changed interpretation?
- Are older studies still relevant to current practice?
- Is the evidence being presented in the historical context in which it was generated?

Older evidence is not automatically invalid.

The concern is **temporal provenance loss**: presenting older evidence as though no later evidence exists.

---

## 9. Preserve Contradictory Evidence

Do not suppress meaningful disagreement simply because a single narrative is easier to teach.

When clinically important evidence conflicts:

- identify the disagreement;
- explain the relevant differences in populations, interventions, outcomes, methods, or interpretations;
- calibrate the educational conclusion accordingly.

---

## 10. Record the Evidence Link

For each major claim, retain enough information for reviewer traceability.

Suggested structure:

| Claim | Source | Evidence Type | Direct/Indirect | Key Limitations | Educational Use |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Exact proposition | Verified source | RCT/guideline/etc. | Direct | Relevant limitations | How it informs teaching |

---

## 11. Final Verification Questions

Before assigning a source to a claim, ask:

1. Does the source exist?
2. Did I review the relevant source content?
3. Does it support the whole claim?
4. Am I preserving the source's population and context?
5. Is the wording proportional to the evidence?
6. Am I preserving the evidence type?
7. Am I introducing an inference?
8. Is the evidence current enough for this teaching point?
9. Is there important contradictory evidence?
10. Would an independent reviewer reach a similar interpretation?

If not, revise the claim or continue evidence identification.

---

## Core Rule

**Citation presence is not equivalent to evidence support.**

The standard is:

**Claim → Verified Source → Evidence Type → Strength and Limitations → Interpretation → Educational Application**
