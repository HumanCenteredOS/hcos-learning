# Epistemic Provenance Standard

## Purpose

Epistemic provenance is the preservation of the origin, type, strength, limitations, interpretation, and uncertainty of knowledge as evidence is transformed into educational content.

The central question is:

> What is known, how is it known, how strongly is it supported, how is it being interpreted, and what remains uncertain?

Educational simplification should improve understanding without erasing these distinctions.

---

## 1. Provenance Categories

### Established Evidence

Findings supported by sufficiently mature and consistent evidence.

Do not use this label merely because a statement is widely repeated.

### Guideline Recommendation

A recommendation issued by a professional organization or authoritative guideline body.

Preserve attribution.

Preferred form:

> `[Organization/guideline] recommends... [REF]`

A guideline recommendation is not automatically equivalent to an independently established scientific fact.

### Primary Research Finding

A finding demonstrated within a specific study or defined body of research.

Preserve relevant limitations involving:

- population;
- intervention;
- comparator;
- outcomes;
- duration;
- setting;
- study design.

### Consensus or Expert Opinion

Professional judgment used when evidence is incomplete, indirect, or evolving.

Do not convert consensus into established evidence.

### Emerging Evidence

Potentially important evidence that is not sufficiently mature to represent established practice.

### Clinical Interpretation

A conclusion produced by applying available evidence to a particular clinical situation.

### Educational Synthesis

An explanation generated by integrating multiple sources for teaching purposes.

Do not present synthesis as though it originated directly from a source.

### Inference

A reasonable conclusion extending beyond what the available evidence directly demonstrates.

Important inferences should be identified when they materially affect clinical interpretation.

### Uncertainty or Evidence Gap

An area in which available evidence does not justify a confident conclusion.

Do not eliminate uncertainty merely to produce a cleaner narrative.

---

## 2. Provenance-Preserving Language

Match wording to the evidence.

Examples:

### Stronger Evidence

- demonstrates;
- was associated with a clinically meaningful reduction when directly supported;
- is recommended by [organization];
- consistent evidence supports.

### More Limited Evidence

- suggests;
- may be associated with;
- has been observed;
- limited evidence indicates;
- emerging evidence suggests;
- expert consensus favors;
- remains uncertain.

Avoid certainty words when the evidence does not justify them.

---

## 3. AI-Specific Provenance Risks

When AI assists with drafting, summarization, organization, or synthesis, actively check for:

### Source Compression

Several distinct findings become one generalized statement.

### Evidence-Strength Inflation

Tentative evidence becomes definitive language.

### Attribution Loss

A recommendation, opinion, or interpretation becomes an unattributed fact.

### Context Loss

Evidence becomes detached from the population, intervention, comparator, setting, timeframe, or limitations in which it was established.

### Inference Laundering

An AI-generated inference is presented as though it originated in a source.

### Consensus Inflation

Several agreeing sources are characterized as universal scientific consensus.

### Temporal Provenance Loss

Older evidence is presented without considering later evidence or guideline changes.

### Contradiction Suppression

Conflicting findings disappear during synthesis.

### Narrative Corroboration Spillover

Evidence supporting one part of an explanation increases confidence in other parts that were not independently established.

---

## 4. Evidence Boundary Protection

Do not automatically transform:

**Association → Causation**

**Correlation → Mechanism**

**Single study → Scientific consensus**

**Surrogate endpoint → Demonstrated patient benefit**

**Statistical significance → Clinical significance**

**Biological plausibility → Demonstrated clinical effectiveness**

**Absence of evidence → Evidence of absence**

**Guideline recommendation → Universal requirement**

**Expert opinion → Established evidence**

**Common clinical practice → Evidence-based best practice**

**Educational synthesis → Source-established conclusion**

**Multiple compatible sources → Proof of the entire narrative**

---

## 5. Preserve Disagreement

When credible sources disagree:

- identify the disagreement;
- preserve relevant differences in methods or populations;
- avoid forcing the evidence into artificial consensus;
- explain what remains clinically actionable despite uncertainty.

A coherent educational story is not necessarily an evidence-supported story.

---

## 6. Provenance Audit

For each major teaching point ask:

1. What exactly is being claimed?
2. How would we know this?
3. What type of evidence supports it?
4. Does the evidence support the whole statement?
5. Is the wording proportional to evidence strength?
6. Has association become causation?
7. Has expert opinion become established evidence?
8. Has a recommendation become an unattributed fact?
9. Has uncertainty been removed?
10. Has an inference been introduced?
11. Have contradictory findings been preserved?
12. Has corroboration of one component increased confidence in unrelated components?
13. Can a reviewer trace the teaching point back to the necessary evidence?
14. Does the teaching point contain multiple claims requiring independent traceability?

---

## 7. Failure Handling

If a claim cannot be adequately traced to supporting evidence:

`[SOURCE VERIFICATION REQUIRED]`

Do not repair a provenance failure by generating a plausible citation.

---

## Core Standard

Educational content should preserve:

**Evidence Origin + Evidence Type + Evidence Strength + Context + Limitations + Interpretation + Uncertainty**

through every stage of transformation from source material to learner-facing education.
